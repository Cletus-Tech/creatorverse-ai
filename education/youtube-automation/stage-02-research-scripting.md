# YouTube Automation — Stage 2: Research, Topic Selection and Scripting
**Duration:** 4 weeks | 6 hours/week | Beginner–Intermediate  
**Outcome:** Learners can research responsibly, select a useful topic using evidence, document claims and sources, and write an original production-ready script.

**Unique artwork brief:** A cinematic creator research desk with a laptop displaying a topic map, handwritten storyboard cards, source notes, headphones and microphone under subtle blue studio lighting. This must be unique to this course and must not reuse another course's image.

## Delivery plan
- Week 1: Research questions, sources and evidence.
- Week 2: Topic ideation, validation and selection.
- Week 3: Script architecture and writing for viewer retention.
- Week 4: Integrated research-to-script lab and assessment.

Weekly rhythm: 60 min educator lesson, 60 min demonstration, 90 min guided lab, 90 min independent work, 60 min review and revision. Provide downloadable notes and offline worksheets for low-bandwidth learners.

# Chapter 1 — Research questions, sources and evidence
**Time:** 60 min teaching, 60 min demonstration, 90 min guided lab, 90 min independent assignment, 30 min review.

### Learning outcomes
Learners can turn a broad subject into a focused research question; separate fact, interpretation, opinion and speculation; prioritise primary and authoritative sources; corroborate important claims; maintain a source log; and recognise when expert review is required.

### Teaching notes

#### 1. Begin with the viewer's question
Research is not collecting many links. It is answering a defined question for a particular viewer. Start with the audience profile and topic brief. Write one central question, then break it into smaller questions: What must the viewer understand? What must be true for the advice to work? What exceptions or risks matter? What might be misunderstood?

Example: Instead of “Android storage”, ask “How can a first-time Android user identify what is using storage without deleting important files?” The narrower question determines what evidence is needed and which advice would be unsafe.

#### 2. Classify statements
- Verifiable fact: can be checked against reliable evidence.
- Interpretation: explains what evidence may mean; show the reasoning.
- Opinion: a judgement; label it and explain the basis.
- Prediction: a claim about what may happen; communicate uncertainty.
- Anecdote: an individual experience; do not present it as universal proof.
- Promotional claim: may have a commercial incentive; independently verify it.

A confident tone does not turn an unsupported claim into a fact. Avoid “always”, “never”, “guaranteed” and “the only way” unless evidence genuinely supports them.

#### 3. Source hierarchy and source fitness
Prefer sources closest to the original evidence:
1. Primary sources: official documentation, original datasets, laws, product documentation, direct statements and original research.
2. Reputable secondary sources: established publications or experts who explain and contextualise primary evidence.
3. Tertiary summaries: useful for orientation, not sufficient for high-impact claims.
4. Social posts, forums and comments: useful for discovering questions and experiences, but factual claims must be verified elsewhere.

Evaluate a source: Who produced it? What expertise or authority do they have? What evidence is shown? When was it published or updated? What incentive or bias may affect it? Does it support the exact statement you intend to make?

For fast-changing topics, record the date checked and recheck near publication. For medical, legal, financial, safety or other high-stakes topics, use authoritative specialist sources and qualified review. Beginners should not present personalised professional advice.

#### 4. Corroboration and claim checking
For important claims, seek an independent second source. Two articles repeating the same press release are not independent corroboration. Trace claims back to the original document or dataset where possible. If sources disagree, explain the uncertainty, narrow the claim or omit it.

Use a claim ledger:

| ID | Draft claim | Source and exact location | Date checked | Independent confirmation | Confidence | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C01 | Claim in plain language | URL, page/section or timestamp | Date | Second source or none | High/medium/low | Keep/rewrite/omit |

Confidence is an editorial judgement, not a scientific measurement. High confidence requires direct, relevant and current evidence. If evidence is weak, remove the claim or explicitly frame it as uncertain.

#### 5. Research notes, paraphrasing and rights
Write notes in your own words and preserve the source reference beside each note. Do not copy paragraphs into a script and lightly change words. Use quotations only when necessary, attributed and within applicable copyright rules. Prefer original explanation. A clip, chart, music track or image being downloadable does not mean it is free to reuse.

#### 6. Research workflow
1. Write the audience question.
2. Define scope and exclusions.
3. List the claims the video needs to make.
4. Find primary sources first.
5. Record source details and publication/update dates.
6. Verify each important claim.
7. Identify gaps, contradictions and risks.
8. Decide: proceed, narrow, request expert review or abandon.
9. Create a research summary with an evidence trail.

### Educator demonstration
Use a low-risk example such as preparing a smartphone for a long trip. Demonstrate forming a central question, identifying official support pages, recording version/date, extracting only what the source says, checking device/version differences and writing a safe, qualified explanation. Show a claim that must be rewritten because the evidence is narrower than the original wording.

### Guided lab
Learners receive a sample brief and mixed-quality sources. In pairs, classify source types, identify unsupported claims, locate a primary source, create a five-row claim ledger and decide which claims to keep, rewrite or omit.

### Independent assignment — Evidence Pack
Choose a topic from the approved niche. Submit:
- Focused viewer question and scope.
- At least five sources, including two primary/official sources where appropriate.
- Claim ledger with at least eight material claims.
- One-page research summary in original words.
- Risk/uncertainty note and proceed/narrow/omit decision.

**Rubric / 25:** question and scope 4; source quality and diversity 6; claim verification 6; source log completeness 4; balanced uncertainty 3; originality and clarity 2. Pass: 18/25. A fabricated source or materially misleading high-impact claim requires revision regardless of score.

### Knowledge check and answers
1. Is a popular social post automatically a reliable factual source? **No; it can reveal questions, but claims need verification.**
2. What is a primary source? **An original document, dataset, official record, direct evidence or original research.**
3. Why do two articles repeating one press release not count as independent confirmation? **They share the same underlying source.**
4. What should happen when strong sources disagree? **Investigate, qualify the conclusion or omit the claim.**
5. What is a claim ledger for? **Tracing each important script claim to evidence and an editorial decision.**
6. Can a source be reputable but unsuitable? **Yes; it may be outdated, irrelevant to the exact claim or outside the source's expertise.**

### Common mistakes and corrections
- Treating search-result snippets as proof: open and inspect the source.
- Generalising one anecdote: label it as an example, not universal evidence.
- Using old interface instructions: check current official documentation and version differences.
- Leaving claims without traceable notes: assign claim IDs and source locations.
- Researching endlessly: set scope and a timebox, then decide if evidence is sufficient.

### Key vocabulary
Primary source, secondary source, corroboration, claim ledger, evidence trail, scope, uncertainty, attribution, editorial review.

# Chapter 2 — Topic ideation, validation and selection
**Time:** 55 min teaching, 60 min demonstration, 90 min guided lab, 90 min assignment, 35 min review.

### Learning outcomes
Learners can generate ideas from audience needs; distinguish demand signals from guaranteed performance; evaluate idea fit, competition, freshness and feasibility; prioritise ideas transparently; and create a complete video brief.

### Teaching notes

#### 1. Idea sources
Build an idea bank from repeated audience questions, search suggestions, comments and public community discussions, official updates, public datasets, gaps in existing explanations, follow-up questions from previous videos, and seasonal needs. Record source and date. Do not harvest private-group data or expose personal details. Each idea record should include the audience question, evidence signal, possible angle, freshness, risk and next research action.

#### 2. Demand is not a promise
Search interest, comments, competitor views and trend charts are clues, not forecasts. Views depend on audience fit, packaging, viewer satisfaction, timing, competition, channel history and many other factors. A high-demand topic may be unsuitable if the creator cannot explain it accurately or produce it well.

Google Trends shows relative interest, not guaranteed video views. Search suggestions reveal phrasing but do not certify demand. Competitor views are historical outcomes for that channel, not a forecast for yours.

#### 3. Competitor and gap analysis
Select a small sample of relevant videos and compare:
- Who is the intended viewer?
- What question does the video answer?
- What does it explain clearly?
- What is missing, outdated, confusing or unsupported?
- Do comments reveal unresolved questions?
- Can your channel add a genuinely useful angle, demonstration, language level or update?

Do not copy scripts, thumbnails, branding or distinctive presentation. A content gap is an opportunity to add value, not permission to imitate.

#### 4. Weighted topic scorecard
Score each idea from 1–5:
- Audience relevance — 25%.
- Evidence of demand — 20%.
- Clear useful outcome — 20%.
- Original angle or content gap — 15%.
- Research and production feasibility — 10%.
- Timeliness or evergreen value — 10%.

Weighted score = sum of (rating ÷ 5 × weight). Add a separate risk flag for rights, safety, factual uncertainty, sensitive topics or high-stakes advice. A high score never overrides an unacceptable risk.

#### 5. Topic formats and series
Possible formats include tutorial, explainer, comparison, case study, myth check, walkthrough, troubleshooting guide, review and story. Choose the format that best fulfils the viewer's need. Each episode in a series must still provide a complete useful outcome. Do not split one simple answer into unnecessary parts just to increase uploads.

#### 6. One-page video brief
Every selected idea needs:
- Working title and central viewer question.
- Intended viewer and current knowledge.
- One-sentence promise and measurable viewer outcome.
- Unique angle and why this channel can deliver it.
- Key claims and research sources.
- Proposed structure and visual proof/demonstration.
- Rights-safe asset plan.
- Risks, limitations and necessary disclosures.
- Relevant, non-manipulative call to action.

### Educator demonstration
Generate ten ideas from one audience question. Use search suggestions, an official update page and public viewer questions as signals. Score three ideas, explain assumptions and choose one. Convert it into a brief with a clear scope and production feasibility check.

### Guided lab
Each learner creates 12 ideas from at least three signal types. In groups, challenge assumptions, score the top five and select one. Explain why the idea is useful even if it does not become popular.

### Independent assignment — 30-Idea Bank and Priority Brief
Submit a 30-idea bank with audience question, signal/source, date, proposed angle and risk note. Score the top ten, justify the top three, and write one complete video brief.

**Rubric / 25:** audience alignment 5; evidence signals 4; originality and gap analysis 4; scoring rationale 5; brief completeness 5; risk awareness 2. Pass: 18/25.

### Knowledge check and answers
1. Does a trend chart predict your video's views? **No; it is one demand signal only.**
2. Why date idea signals? **To judge freshness and repeatability.**
3. What is a content gap? **A meaningful unmet need or weakness in available explanations.**
4. Can a high topic score override an unsafe or unsupported claim? **No.**
5. What is the purpose of a video brief? **To align viewer, promise, evidence, angle, structure and production plan.**

### Common mistakes
Chasing unrelated trends; copying the most-viewed competitor; treating a score as certainty; choosing before checking feasibility; making vague promises. Correct each by prioritising audience fit, adding original value, recording assumptions, confirming evidence/skills/assets and defining the viewer outcome.

### Key vocabulary
Idea bank, demand signal, search intent, competitor analysis, content gap, evergreen topic, timely topic, weighted score, video brief.

# Chapter 3 — Script architecture and writing for viewer retention
**Time:** 65 min teaching, 60 min demonstration, 90 min guided writing lab, 90 min assignment, 35 min review.

### Learning outcomes
Learners can write a script that fulfils a clear promise; create an honest opening; organise information logically; use examples, transitions and visual cues; remove filler; write a satisfying ending; and check accuracy, originality and audience fit.

### Teaching notes

#### 1. A script is a guided viewer experience
The purpose is not to keep a person watching at any cost. It is to help the intended viewer understand or accomplish something. Retention is useful feedback, but manipulative suspense, false urgency and withholding the promised answer damage trust.

Ask: What does the viewer need first? What must they understand next? Where might they become confused? What example proves the idea? What should they be able to do at the end?

#### 2. Opening: relevance, promise and proof
A strong opening establishes the problem or question, why it matters to this viewer, what the video will help them understand or do, and a reason to trust the explanation (a tested demonstration, transparent method or credible evidence).

Avoid long greetings, unrelated introductions, exaggerated claims and promises the video cannot fulfil. Never pretend to have tested something you have not.

Example: “Running out of phone storage can make updates and photos difficult. In this walkthrough, we'll identify what's using space and show safe ways to free it without deleting unknown system files.”

#### 3. Body: sequence and cognitive load
Choose a structure suited to the topic:
- Step-by-step: prerequisites → steps → verification → troubleshooting.
- Explainer: question → concept → example → implication → limitations.
- Comparison: criteria → option A → option B → trade-offs → recommendation by use case.
- Case study: context → challenge → decisions → outcome → lessons.
- List: selection criteria → ordered items → practical explanation → summary.

Teach one major idea at a time. Explain unfamiliar terms before relying on them. Use signposting. Avoid repeating points merely to lengthen the video.

#### 4. Demonstrations and visual writing
Write for the screen, not only for the voice. Add production cues such as:
- [SCREEN RECORDING: open Settings > Storage; interface may vary by device]
- [ON SCREEN: highlight the menu label]
- [DIAGRAM: show the three-step process]
- [B-ROLL: original footage illustrating the point]
- [SOURCE NOTE: official support page, checked on date]

Visuals should clarify or prove the explanation. Do not use unrelated stock footage as a substitute for evidence. If an interface changes by device or software version, say so and demonstrate the tested version.

#### 5. Voice and sentence craft
Write in natural spoken language. Prefer short, direct sentences and concrete verbs. Read aloud to identify awkward phrasing. Explain technical words plainly. Avoid artificial excitement and generic suspense phrases unless the content warrants them.

#### 6. Transitions and open loops
A transition explains why the next section matters. A question may be introduced and answered later when the sequence genuinely benefits understanding. Do not create fake suspense or delay a simple answer. Every section should advance understanding.

#### 7. Ending and call to action
Summarise the useful outcome, mention limitations or next steps, and invite one relevant action: try the method, consult a source or watch a related lesson. Avoid unrelated requests and guaranteed outcomes.

#### 8. Script quality-control checklist
- Does the opening match the actual content?
- Is the viewer and promised outcome clear?
- Is every factual claim supported by the claim ledger?
- Are uncertainty and limitations represented honestly?
- Is the order logical for a beginner?
- Are technical terms explained?
- Do visual directions support the narration?
- Are third-party assets rights-cleared?
- Is the script original rather than a paraphrase of one source?
- Does the ending deliver closure?
- Has it been read aloud and timed?

### Educator demonstration
Take a weak script with a long greeting, unsupported superlatives and scattered steps. Identify the promise, move prerequisites earlier, remove repeated lines, attach source notes, add screen directions and rewrite the ending. Read both versions aloud and compare clarity, not merely word count.

### Guided lab
Learners create a 60–90 second script from their Chapter 2 brief. Partners mark the promise, claim IDs, visual cues, confusing terms and unnecessary lines. Learners revise and read aloud.

### Independent assignment — Production-Ready Script
Submit a 4–7 minute script (or justify another duration), source/claim ledger linked to script sections, visual and audio directions, three accurate title options, rights-safe asset plan and a self-review explaining revisions.

**Rubric / 25:** opening and promise 4; structure and clarity 5; evidence accuracy 5; spoken language 3; useful visual directions 3; originality and rights 3; ending and quality-control evidence 2. Pass: 18/25. Unsupported claims or misleading promises must be corrected.

### Knowledge check and answers
1. What should an opening establish? **Viewer problem, relevance, credible promise and scope.**
2. Why write visual directions? **They make the script producible and ensure visuals support the explanation.**
3. Is retention maximised by delaying every answer? **No; deliver value honestly and use sequence only when it helps understanding.**
4. What should happen to an unsupported claim? **Verify, qualify, rewrite or remove it.**
5. Why read a script aloud? **To test natural speech, pacing and clarity.**
6. What makes a CTA appropriate? **It is relevant, proportionate and connected to the viewer's next useful action.**

### Common mistakes
Overpromising in the hook; writing like an essay; using unrelated visuals; packing too many new ideas into one paragraph; accepting AI-generated scripts without review. Correct through promise alignment, spoken-language editing, purposeful visuals, clear sequencing and claim verification.

### Key vocabulary
Hook, viewer promise, script beat, signposting, transition, open loop, visual cue, call to action, pacing, cognitive load.

# Stage 2 integrated assessment — Research-to-Script Portfolio
Submit one complete evidence pack and production-ready script for a video in the learner's niche: audience question, idea-bank record, topic scorecard, video brief, claim ledger, source log, script with visual cues, rights plan and revision note.

**100-point rubric:** research question and scope 10; source quality and verification 20; topic selection and originality 15; audience promise and brief 10; script structure and spoken clarity 20; visual production directions 10; copyright, safety and uncertainty controls 10; reflection and revision 5.

**Pass:** 70/100, and all critical accuracy, source and rights checks must pass. Otherwise, learner revises the affected section and resubmits.

## Educator marking protocol
Return a score and rubric breakdown; identify two strengths; identify up to three priority corrections; cite the exact claim or script section needing attention; provide a model correction where useful; and assign one next action. Do not rewrite the entire learner submission without explaining the learning point.

## Admin publishing checklist
Before any chapter becomes student-visible, authorised admins must be able to inspect and control course/stage association; title, summary, outcomes and estimated time; full teaching content; educator demonstration notes; guided practice; assignments and expected submissions; knowledge questions, answers and explanations; rubric and pass threshold; media, alt text and usage rights; external references and review dates; draft/review/approved/published status; display order; revision history, restore and archive actions.

**Approval is not publication.** Publishing to the live course requires a separate explicit admin action and final preview.
