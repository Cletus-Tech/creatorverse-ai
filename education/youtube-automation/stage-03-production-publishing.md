# YouTube Automation — Stage 3: Production, Editing and Publishing
**Duration:** 4 weeks | 6 hours/week | Beginner–Intermediate  
**Stage outcome:** Learners can turn an approved script into an original, clear and rights-aware video; complete technical and editorial quality checks; package it with an accurate title and thumbnail; and prepare a responsible upload.

**Unique artwork brief:** A polished video-editing workstation with a timeline, audio waveform, storyboard frames and thumbnail sketch on a large monitor, with a microphone and headphones. Use a visual language distinct from the research desk artwork of Stage 2.

## Delivery plan
- Week 1: Chapter 1 — Narration, screen capture and visual production.
- Week 2: Chapter 2 — Editing workflow, sound, captions and quality assurance.
- Week 3: Chapter 3 — Titles, thumbnails, metadata and responsible publishing.
- Week 4: Integrated production lab and assessment.

## Chapter 1 — Narration, screen capture and visual production
### Learning outcomes
Learners can prepare a recording plan; record clear narration; capture usable screen demonstrations; select or create rights-safe visual assets; and maintain an asset provenance log.

### Teaching notes
**1. Convert the approved script into a shot list.** Break the script into sections and decide what viewers should see for each spoken idea. Common visual types include presenter-free screen capture, original camera footage, diagrams, charts, animation, licensed stock media and text callouts. Every visual should clarify, demonstrate or provide evidence. Decorative footage that distracts from the point should be removed.

**2. Narration preparation.** Choose a quiet space, reduce echo and background noise, keep a consistent microphone distance and record a short test before the full take. Speak naturally and clearly; do not imitate an artificial presenter voice. Record in sections when helpful and leave a little silence at the start and end for editing. Keep original takes and label versions. If using synthetic voice, check the tool's terms, disclosure requirements and rights; never clone a real person's voice without explicit permission.

**3. Screen recording.** Close private messages, account details and unrelated tabs before recording. Use a clean test account or demo data when possible. Increase interface scaling so labels are readable. Move the cursor deliberately, pause before important actions and capture only the steps needed. Record device/software version because interfaces vary. Never expose passwords, tokens, personal records or confidential information.

**4. Visual asset selection.** Preferred order: original assets created for the video; assets with a clearly suitable licence; public-domain material verified for the relevant jurisdiction; or written permission from the rights holder. Check the licence for commercial use, attribution, modification and redistribution restrictions. Save the source, licence terms, creator, date checked and intended use. “Found on Google”, “royalty-free” or “credit given” does not automatically mean permission exists.

**5. Charts and demonstrations.** Cite the source and date for data. Do not distort chart axes or crop context in a misleading way. Explain what a chart measures and what it does not show. When demonstrating a process, test it first and state relevant version or device differences.

### Educator demonstration
Create a shot list from a one-minute script. Record a short voiceover, compare microphone distance and room noise, capture a safe settings walkthrough using demo data, and log a properly licensed visual. Demonstrate a screen capture with private notifications disabled.

### Guided lab
Learners record a 30-second narration, capture one screen demonstration or create an original diagram, and prepare a five-item asset log. Peers assess intelligibility, relevance, privacy and provenance.

### Independent assignment — Production Asset Pack
Submit a shot list, clean narration recording, at least three original or properly licensed visuals, screen capture if relevant, asset provenance log and a note explaining privacy controls.

**Rubric / 25:** narration clarity 5; visual relevance 5; screen-recording quality 4; rights/provenance 6; privacy and version accuracy 5. Pass: 18/25. Any exposed private credentials or unauthorised personal material must be removed before resubmission.

### Knowledge check and answers
1. Does giving credit automatically grant reuse rights? **No; permission or a suitable licence is still needed.**
2. Why keep an asset log? **To prove where assets came from and what use is permitted.**
3. What must be hidden before screen recording? **Passwords, tokens, personal information and confidential material.**
4. Why record the software version? **Interfaces and instructions can differ by version.**
5. What makes a visual useful? **It clarifies, demonstrates or evidences the narration.**

### Common mistakes
Recording before testing audio; capturing notifications; using random search images; assuming credit is permission; using visuals that do not match the spoken explanation. Correct with test recordings, privacy checks, licence verification and shot-list review.

### Key vocabulary
Shot list, voiceover, screen capture, asset provenance, licence, attribution, public domain, demo data, visual evidence.

## Chapter 2 — Editing workflow, sound, captions and quality assurance
### Learning outcomes
Learners can organise a project; assemble a coherent timeline; edit narration and visuals; balance sound; add readable captions; export appropriate files; and complete a repeatable quality-control review.

### Teaching notes
**1. Project organisation.** Keep a master folder with brief, research, script, narration, visuals, project file, exports, thumbnail, metadata and performance notes. Use consistent file names and version numbers. Back up source media and project files. Do not overwrite the only original recording.

**2. Build a rough cut first.** Place narration in the intended order, remove unusable takes and arrange visuals against the spoken explanation. Check that the argument flows before adding effects. A rough cut should make sense even with simple visuals.

**3. Pacing and continuity.** Remove long accidental pauses, repeated sentences and irrelevant detours. Keep enough breathing room for viewers to understand demonstrations. Use transitions only when they help. Avoid rapid cuts, zooms and effects that make text difficult to read or distract from learning.

**4. Sound.** Narration must be intelligible on ordinary phone speakers and headphones. Reduce noise carefully; aggressive noise removal can distort speech. Keep music low enough that words remain clear. Use only music and effects with appropriate rights. Listen with headphones and a phone speaker. Check for clipping, abrupt cuts, hum and volume jumps.

**5. Captions and accessibility.** Correct automatic captions; names, technical terms and numbers are often misrecognised. Use readable contrast, sensible line breaks and synchronisation. Do not rely on colour alone to communicate meaning. Ensure essential information is spoken or otherwise accessible, not only shown briefly on screen. Provide a transcript when practical.

**6. Export and review.** Use the platform's current recommended export settings and check the exported file, not only the editing preview. Confirm aspect ratio, resolution, frame rate, audio sync, black frames, missing media and readable text. Keep a high-quality master and a platform upload copy.

### Educator demonstration
Show a rough timeline becoming a clean edit. Demonstrate trimming a false start, placing a visual cue, lowering background music, correcting captions, exporting a test and watching the exported file on a phone.

### Guided lab
Learners edit a 45–60 second sample. A partner uses the quality checklist to note one clarity issue, one sound issue, one caption issue and one unnecessary edit.

### Independent assignment — Edited Pilot
Submit a 2–4 minute edited video, editable project file, caption file or verified caption track, final export and completed quality-control checklist.

**Rubric / 25:** narrative flow 5; edit precision 4; sound quality 5; captions/accessibility 4; export reliability 4; project organisation 3. Pass: 18/25.

### Knowledge check and answers
1. Why make a rough cut before effects? **To confirm structure and clarity before polishing.**
2. What is audio clipping? **Distortion caused when the signal exceeds the available recording or playback level.**
3. Are automatic captions always correct? **No; they require human correction.**
4. Why inspect the exported file? **Export can reveal problems not visible in the editor preview.**
5. Why keep original media? **For recovery, revisions and future edits.**

### Common mistakes
Overusing transitions; music masking narration; trusting automatic captions; editing only in the preview; keeping one copy of project files. Correct by prioritising structure, checking sound on multiple devices, correcting captions and keeping backups.

### Key vocabulary
Timeline, rough cut, fine cut, continuity, audio clipping, noise reduction, captions, transcript, export, master file.

## Chapter 3 — Titles, thumbnails, metadata and responsible publishing
### Learning outcomes
Learners can create accurate titles and thumbnails; understand basic metadata; prepare a complete upload checklist; select audience settings responsibly; and avoid misleading or policy-violating packaging.

### Teaching notes
**1. Packaging is a promise.** A title and thumbnail help viewers decide whether a video is relevant. They should be compelling but accurate. The video must fulfil the expectation created by the packaging. Avoid fabricated results, fake screenshots, false urgency and sensational claims unsupported by the content.

**2. Title writing.** Lead with the viewer's question, task or outcome. Be specific and concise. Include important terms naturally; do not stuff keywords. Create several options and test them against the script: Would a reasonable viewer understand what the video actually covers? Does the title imply a result the creator cannot guarantee?

**3. Thumbnail design.** Use one clear visual idea, strong subject hierarchy, readable text at small size and contrast that works on a phone. Avoid clutter and tiny paragraphs. Do not use another creator's thumbnail or imply a person endorsed the video when they did not. Check the thumbnail at small display size.

**4. Metadata.** Write a useful description that summarises the video, includes relevant context and links to sources where appropriate. Add chapters only when they help navigation and match the actual video. Use tags accurately; do not expect tags to rescue weak content. Select language, category, captions, audience designation and other settings carefully. Do not mark content “made for kids” or “not made for kids” casually; follow the platform's current criteria and applicable law.

**5. Upload and publish checklist.** Check the final video, title, thumbnail, description, source links, captions, audience designation, visibility, comments settings and any required disclosures. Confirm rights for all assets. Choose private/unlisted review when needed. Schedule only when ready. Reopen the published page to verify playback, captions, thumbnail and links.

**6. Policy and transparency.** Follow current Community Guidelines, advertiser-friendly guidelines and disclosure requirements. Disclose sponsorships, affiliate relationships and material connections clearly. Synthetic or altered content may require disclosure under current platform rules; check official requirements for each upload. Never buy views, subscribers or engagement, and never use misleading metadata.

### Educator demonstration
Take one completed pilot and produce three accurate title options, two thumbnail sketches and a description. Compare how each package changes the viewer expectation. Complete a mock upload checklist and explain the audience and disclosure settings.

### Guided lab
Learners create two thumbnail concepts and five titles for their edited pilot. Peers identify the strongest accurate promise and flag any misleading implication. Learners revise and complete metadata.

### Independent assignment — Publish-Ready Package
Submit the final video; three title options and selected title; two thumbnail concepts and final thumbnail; description; chapters if useful; verified captions; rights log; audience/disclosure decision; and signed upload checklist.

**Rubric / 25:** title accuracy and clarity 5; thumbnail hierarchy/readability 5; description and metadata 4; audience/disclosure settings 4; rights and policy checks 5; final verification 2. Pass: 18/25. Misleading packaging or unresolved rights issues require revision.

### Knowledge check and answers
1. What is the relationship between title and video? **The title sets an expectation that the video must fulfil.**
2. Should tags compensate for weak content? **No.**
3. Why inspect a thumbnail at phone size? **Most viewers may see it small; readability and hierarchy matter.**
4. When should sponsorship or affiliate relationships be disclosed? **Clearly, when the relationship exists and according to current rules and law.**
5. Is synthetic media disclosure optional in every case? **No; check current platform rules and applicable requirements.**
6. What should happen after publishing? **Verify playback, captions, thumbnail, description and links.**

### Common mistakes
Clickbait; too much thumbnail text; keyword stuffing; incorrect audience settings; missing disclosures; assuming upload completion means everything displays correctly. Correct through promise checks, small-screen review, accurate metadata, careful settings and post-publish verification.

### Key vocabulary
Packaging, title promise, thumbnail hierarchy, metadata, chapters, visibility, audience designation, disclosure, click-through rate.

## Stage 3 integrated assessment — Finished Pilot Video
Submit a complete 3–6 minute pilot video, editable project, narration, asset log, captions, title/thumbnail package, description and upload checklist.

**100-point rubric:** narration and visuals 15; editing and story clarity 20; audio and captions 15; asset rights and privacy 15; title/thumbnail accuracy 15; metadata and settings 10; final technical quality and verification 10. Pass: 70/100 and all rights/privacy/policy safeguards must pass.

## Admin publishing checklist
Admins must be able to edit the stage/chapter content, learning outcomes, lesson notes, demonstration steps, shot-list template, asset log template, editing checklist, assignment, sample submissions, quiz questions and answers, rubric, media/image, alt text, reference links and review dates, sequence and estimated time. Admins must separately control draft/review/approved/published status, revision history, restore and archive. Approval must not auto-publish.
